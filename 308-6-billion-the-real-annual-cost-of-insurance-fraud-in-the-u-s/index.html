Insurance fraud / industry data

$308.6 billion:
the annual cost of insurance fraud.

A closer look at the Coalition Against Insurance Fraud’s 2022 national estimate, the insurance lines included in the research and why broad industry data still requires careful claim-level analysis.

National insurance fraud estimate

The Coalition Against Insurance Fraud reported in 2022 that insurance fraud costs United States consumers and businesses an estimated $308.6 billion annually. The organization described the study as the first comprehensive update to the national estimate in 27 years.

The figure is significant not only because it is larger than the longstanding estimate, but because the newer analysis considers more insurance lines. The earlier national figure, released in 1995, focused primarily on property and casualty insurance and several related categories. The 2022 estimate extends across a broader range that includes life, health, workers’ compensation and auto theft.

What the updated estimate includes

The research reports that property-and-casualty fraud accounts for an estimated $45 billion each year, excluding auto theft and workers’ compensation because those categories were analyzed separately. Auto-theft fraud was estimated at $7.4 billion annually.

Workers’ compensation fraud was estimated at $34 billion annually. The cited breakdown attributes approximately $9 billion to premium fraud and $25 billion to claims fraud. Those categories illustrate why a single national total can encompass very different conduct, evidence and legal frameworks.

A national estimate is context—not proof about a claim. Industry research can help explain scale and recurring risk, but it does not establish that a particular bill, provider, claimant or transaction is fraudulent. A legal assessment must remain tied to the complete record.

Why the number changed

The research reflects changes in both methodology and the insurance environment. The earlier estimate had not been updated for inflation, and it arose before internet-based commerce and digital claims systems became central to insurance operations. The 2022 project considered more lines of business and a much broader modern risk landscape.

The Coalition framed the updated estimate as an awareness tool for consumers, insurers, regulators and lawmakers. Its practical value lies in showing that fraud-related costs are distributed across many types of insurance rather than confined to one line of business.

From industry data to an evidence-driven matter

For insurers and Special Investigation Units, aggregate data is only the beginning. A specific matter may require coordinated review of claims data, treatment records, billing patterns, ownership information, referral relationships, communications and financial records. The relevant question is not whether fraud exists at a national scale; it is what the available evidence supports in the matter at hand.

Kennedy Vuernick’s Insurance Fraud Litigation practice is structured around that distinction. The firm’s approach connects records that may appear ordinary in isolation, tests the developing theory against applicable law and helps clients evaluate an appropriate investigative or litigation path.
