Practice overview

Evidence before conclusions

Understand the transaction.
Protect the record.

Fraud against an individual can involve a stranger, a professional adviser, a caregiver, a family member or another person in a position of trust. Victims may discover the problem through an unexplained transfer, changed account access, a questionable investment, a document they did not understand or a pattern of pressure and isolation.

The legal analysis begins with a reliable chronology. Kennedy Vuernick works to identify what the individual was told, what authority was granted, how money or property moved and which records can confirm the events. That process is especially important when health, age, dependency or a confidential relationship affects the circumstances.

A civil response may involve preserving assets, seeking information, challenging transactions, asserting fraud or fiduciary claims, or coordinating with appropriate authorities. The right approach depends on proof, available remedies, timing and the client’s immediate needs.

This page provides general information and is not legal advice. It does not evaluate any specific person, transaction, investigation or dispute.

Issues that may require review

Different schemes can share
the same warning signs.

A warning sign is not proof. It is a reason to preserve information and examine the circumstances before more assets or evidence disappear.

01

Misuse of entrusted authority

A person with account access, a power of attorney or another position of confidence may act outside permitted purposes.

02

Investment or transaction deception

Material facts may be concealed or misstated to induce a transfer, purchase, loan or investment.

03

Caregiver or family exploitation

Dependency and trust may be used to obtain money, property, access or changes in control.

04

Impersonation and account takeover

Fraudsters may use false identities, urgent demands or stolen credentials to redirect funds.

Scope / evidence / analysis / strategy

Evidence-driven development

Follow the authority.
Follow the money.

Chronology. Build a clear sequence of representations, decisions, transfers and changes in access or control.

Authority. Examine agreements, powers, account permissions and duties governing the people involved.

Financial path. Trace payments, withdrawals, asset transfers and the recipients or accounts that benefited.

Corroboration. Use communications, witnesses, institutional records and other evidence to test competing explanations.

A deliberate process

Move carefully
without losing time.

Individual fraud matters can involve urgent financial and family concerns. The response should protect evidence while avoiding unsupported accusations.

  1. 01

    Identify immediate risk

    Determine whether assets, access, documents or vulnerable people face continuing harm that requires prompt attention.

  2. 02

    Preserve and collect

    Secure statements, agreements, messages, account records and other information before it is altered or lost.

  3. 03

    Assess claims and forums

    Evaluate potential parties, legal theories, equitable relief, reporting options and practical recovery considerations.

  4. 04

    Choose a proportionate response

    Pursue investigation, demand, negotiation or litigation based on the evidence and the client’s goals.

Legal and practical context

Financial exploitation can involve
overlapping legal questions.

A suspected scheme may implicate fraud, breach of fiduciary duty, conversion, contract, undue influence or other legal principles. The available claims and remedies depend on the relationship, transaction and evidence.

Some situations also involve banks, advisers, healthcare providers, government benefits, estate documents or law-enforcement reporting. Coordinating those issues requires a clear understanding of what each process can—and cannot—accomplish.

Kennedy Vuernick focuses on the investigative and litigation record. When a matter requires another discipline, the firm can help identify the need for coordinated advice without overstating its role.

General information

Fraud Against Individuals
questions.

These answers provide a general orientation. They do not assess a particular matter or create an attorney-client relationship.

What information should an individual preserve?

Preserve account statements, contracts, checks, wire details, emails, text messages, letters, recordings lawfully possessed and notes identifying relevant people and dates.

What if the suspected fraud involves someone the person trusted?

The relationship may affect legal duties, access to evidence and the practical response. Counsel can evaluate authority and transactions without assuming that trust alone proves wrongdoing.

Can assets be protected while a case is investigated?

Certain facts may support urgent or equitable relief, but courts require specific evidence and legal standards. The options should be evaluated promptly.

Should suspected fraud also be reported to law enforcement?

Reporting may be appropriate in some matters, but it does not replace civil advice and may affect strategy. Counsel can discuss the relevant options based on the facts.
