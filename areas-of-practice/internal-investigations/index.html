Practice overview

Evidence before conclusions

Independence begins
with a clear mandate.

An internal investigation must be credible to the people who will rely on it. That requires more than collecting documents or interviewing a few witnesses. The organization needs a defined scope, appropriate independence, a reliable process and conclusions tied to evidence.

Kennedy Vuernick helps decision-makers identify the questions the investigation must answer, the authority under which it will proceed and the people responsible for receiving the results. The firm then develops a work plan proportionate to the issue, preserving flexibility as new facts emerge.

The goal is the unvarnished record: what occurred, which policies or obligations may be implicated, where evidence is incomplete and what practical options the governing body should consider. The investigation should not begin with a predetermined outcome.

Legal and practical context

Investigations and monitorships are
related but distinct functions.

An internal investigation seeks to establish facts and advise an organization or governing body. A monitorship or other independent oversight assignment operates under a specific agreement, order or mandate and may involve ongoing observation, testing and reporting.

The scope, authority, confidentiality and reporting obligations must be defined at the outset. An independent professional should not imply powers beyond those actually granted.

Monitoring terms should identify what must be tested, which records may be reviewed, how exceptions are handled, who receives reports and how long the work continues. Those boundaries help preserve independence and keep the engagement proportionate.

General information

Internal Investigations & Monitorships
questions.

These answers provide a general orientation. They do not assess a particular matter or create an attorney-client relationship.

Who should authorize an internal investigation?

The appropriate decision-maker may be a board, committee, executive, public body or counsel, depending on the allegation and potential conflicts. Clear reporting authority supports independence.

How is the scope of an investigation determined?

Scope should reflect the allegation, legal risk, evidence, affected people and the decisions that must be made. It may be adjusted when credible new information emerges.

What makes an investigation defensible?

A clear mandate, appropriate independence, preserved evidence, fair interviews, documented methods and conclusions tied to the record all support credibility.

How does a monitorship differ from an investigation?

An investigation generally develops facts about defined issues. A monitorship or oversight role may continue over time and measure conduct or implementation against a governing mandate.
